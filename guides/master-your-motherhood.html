Before you start

Mastering here means knowing how to adjust, not getting it finished.

Your family will keep changing. A new baby, a new school year, a diagnosis, a move, a new job. The systems in this book are meant to change with it. When something stops working, that is not a failure. It usually means your season changed.

The chapters build on each other, so we recommend working through them in order. Each one also stands on its own, so you can come back to any topic whenever you need it.

How this workbook handles facts

When something comes from a study, you will see a small number that points to the sources at the back, and the shaded “What the research says” boxes sum up what the studies found. The rest of this book is my own writing, drawn from my research on motherhood and from raising our family. Everything here is a suggestion to test in your own home and keep only if it helps.

This workbook is for education and support. It is not medical or mental health advice, and it cannot diagnose ADHD or any other condition. If you are struggling, please talk with your doctor or a licensed professional.

    The one thing to remember

    The motherhood that society has left us with

    If you are wondering why you feel so lonely, isolated and overwhelmed as a mom, I want you to hear this first: it’s not just you. You aren’t alone, and you aren’t the whole problem. Our society sells us a picture of motherhood that is polished, exhausting and lonely, and then tells us that if we are struggling, we must be doing it wrong.

    We know it is our job to raise kind, good humans. But somewhere along the way, we started believing we have to do it alone, with total self-sacrifice. From my research and my own experience, I believe many families fall into roles based on what they grew up with or what society treats as normal, not on what actually fits them. And that script creates some pretty unhealthy, stressful and heavy roles that lead to strained marriages, isolation and burnout.

    Many cultures still hold on to the idea that “it takes a village”: that raising children is shared work, so that no one mother has to carry it all. So why are we left isolated and alone? Why do we pull into our garages, close the door right behind us and rush inside to jump into the next task?

    I don’t believe families were designed to function alone. We have to do better. And to do better, we have to know better. Just because something is normal doesn’t mean it has to stay that way. That is what this workbook is for: changing the part you can actually change, in your own home.

    Hold both truths

    If you only hear “it’s the system,” you can end up feeling stuck. If you only hear “it’s your mindset,” you end up blaming yourself for things no amount of positive thinking can fix, like the cost of childcare or a job with no flexibility. So this book holds both: name what isn’t yours to carry, then change what is.

    The thinking is the work.

    Asking is the job. Hand over the noticing, not just the doing.

    The one thing to remember

    “Normal” household roles

    In a lot of families, women carry the “emotional burden” of the family. By that I mean the remembering, the planning, the worrying and the keeping track of everything. Our partners (or both of us) provide a steady income and give their time to work, and we are left with the rest. And it is really, really hard.

    We sign the school papers. We schedule the appointments. We do most of the household duties, including the mental load of grocery shopping and meal planning, among a million other tasks. We know who needs new shoes, when the field trip money is due and that we are almost out of toilet paper. And while our partners are doing their part by providing financially (or we both are), the workload, mental and physical, ends up unbalanced. Most of the time, only the physical jobs get divided and discussed: who does the dishes, who takes out the trash. But the mental side of running a family is just as exhausting, if not more.

    We’ve all heard our sweet-hearted spouses say, “If you need help, all you have to do is ask.” That is so kind. But here is the deal.

    It is a job in itself to be the only one who has to think through everything that needs to be done and then hand out the actual tasks. So we end up stuck in a funky situation. We know they aren’t mind readers, but we don’t want to walk someone through every single request, either. So we decide to just do it ourselves. After all, that is less work than all the other options, right?

    Wrong.

    Doing it all ourselves keeps the load exactly where it is: on us. I believe there is a different approach, one that can relieve a lot of the stress most moms feel. We can live an entirely different life than the one society says “works.” It might work for some, but for most moms it is impossible to succeed at, especially in America, where the role of “mom” is one of the most undervalued roles out there.

    One simple change can create HUGE waves of change: start communicating clearly and let your family step up into roles that serve everyone better. Not just “helping mom,” but owning parts of the work, start to finish. Create a home where everyone brings their own skills to the table to serve each other in the best way possible!

    Just a note: I can’t speak for 100% of homes. These statements come from statistics and from our own experience and that of close friends and family. If they don’t totally fit your circumstances, that is okay. Take what fits, leave what doesn’t, and figure out what works best for you and your family.

    What the research says

    A 2019 study of 35 couples found that women did more of this thinking work, especially anticipating what the family would need and keeping track of whether things got done. Making the final decisions was closer to equal.9 It was a small interview study, so it shows how the work gets divided, not how common that pattern is everywhere.

    The four parts of every job

    Every area of family life has four parts. Most families split the third one and leave the other three with one person. That is why “just ask me” doesn’t help. Asking is the job.

    1. Notice

    Seeing that something is needed: the sneakers are tight, the forms came home.

    2. Plan

    Working out what, when and how: which store, which size, by when.

    3. Do

    The part everyone can see: buying the shoes, signing the form.

    4. Follow up

    Checking it got done, and noticing what comes next.

    How to hand over an area

    1. Hand over a whole area, all four parts. Not “buy the shoes,” but “you own kids’ clothes and sizes.”
    2. Write a handoff note once: what you know, where things are, what’s coming up. There is a template in Chapter 3.
    3. Agree what “done” means, then let their way be different from yours.
    4. Don’t take it back on the first dropped ball. Talk about it at the next family meeting instead.
    Example

    Handing over school paperwork

    A made-up family: Mom, Dad and two school-age kids. School paperwork means forms, fees, permission slips, picture day and report cards.

    The handoff note they wrote

    The area: School paperwork for both kids.

    What it includes: Forms, fees, permission slips, picture day, report cards. All four parts.

    Coming up in the next month: Field trip form due the 14th. Fall pictures on the 22nd.

    Where things are: The school app (the login is in our shared password manager), the folder in each backpack, teacher emails in the family inbox.

    What “done” looks like: Signed, paid and returned by the due date, without a reminder from me.

    What I will stop doing: Checking backpacks and asking, “Did you sign it?”

    If ADHD is in your house

    If you have ADHD, you may be both the one carrying the load and the one dropping balls. Both can be true. Choose areas that fit how your brain works, and ask for help with the slow, repeating ones. If your partner or child has ADHD, hand over areas with reminders built in: a recurring phone alert, or a checklist taped where the job happens.

    Helping is not the same as owning. Ask for ownership.

    The one thing to remember

    A letter from Stuart: his perspective on a man’s role in the home

    When I think about household responsibilities and the roles we all play, I automatically flash back to being a child and what was expected of the kids in the house. Those experiences have shaped the way I see household responsibilities today.

    It is interesting to see how roles have changed with each generation.

    As a young boy, I watched both of my grandmas selflessly serve their families. As grandchildren, we weren’t expected to set the table, help with the food, clear the table, clean up or anything else. They would literally serve the entire time. Part of me felt bad watching them work so tirelessly for their family. But seeing that selflessness made me want to step up and help.

    Before my parents divorced, the household duties were shared better than in the generation before, but things still felt slightly off balance. Once they divorced, they both cooked, cleaned, ironed and helped out where needed in their own homes. Seeing that both of my parents were completely capable made me believe that roles and responsibilities could be more balanced than they were in the generations before. It helped me step into the father I am today.

    After the divorce, I lived mostly with my mother. Growing up in a single-parent home, we all had to pull our own weight and get things done together. I remember my preteen years: always cutting the grass on the weekends and cleaning the car. There may have been incentives for those jobs, but I also knew I had a part to play in my family.

    As I got into my teen years, I added to my skill set. I did my own laundry, ironed my clothes, loaded and unloaded the dishwasher and started helping with handiwork around the house. I quickly learned that there isn’t much to using a washer, dryer, iron or dishwasher. It was simply a task that needed to be done.

    Now, as an adult with a family of my own, I have carried that expectation with me: we are all capable, and we should all help run the house, regardless of gender, age, marital status or work schedule. You are able to help, and you should be helping.

    Take dinnertime, for example. I believe that if you cook it, you don’t dare clean it. Children are more than capable of clearing the table, scraping the dishes and putting things away, just as husbands and wives are more than capable of working together to take stress off each other.

    Are there things men might be better at, or prefer to do? Sure.

    Are there things women might be better at, or prefer to do? Sure.

    There are also things that both men and women don’t enjoy doing. But a team effort helps balance that out. Communication and humility will take you far.

    And assumptions only lead to resentment and frustration.

    The 2am story

    Here is a story I like to use to show what I mean. A husband and wife are lying in bed when their 1-year-old starts crying in the middle of the night. It’s 2am. They are both awake, lying back to back, and neither one knows the other is awake.

    The husband is thinking, “I have been at work all day, and I have to get up in four hours to go back. Surely she can hear the baby. When is she going to get up?”

    Meanwhile, the wife is thinking, “I have been with the kids all day, and I have to get up in four hours and do it all over again. Surely he can hear the baby. When is he going to get up?”

    This is a dangerous place to be. And how common is it?

    If nothing changes, this will quickly turn into resentment toward each other. So who is right in this scenario? Neither of them. They are both exhausted, they both have a point, and neither of them said a word.

    Now picture the same night with a plan. On Sunday, at the family meeting, they agreed that he takes the wake-ups on weeknights, because she is up early with the kids, and she takes them on weekends. The baby cries, he gets up, and nobody lies awake keeping score. Same baby, same exhaustion, no resentment. That is the power of saying it out loud ahead of time.

    Serving each other is about humbling yourself and putting the other person first sometimes. That is essentially what a family is: people working together and serving one another for something bigger than any one of them.

    So don’t assume, or expect, that someone else will pick up the slack because you don’t want to do something or you feel it is their job. Talk about it. Work together, serve one another and, believe me, you will both quickly learn that helping out actually feels good.

    “You, my brothers and sisters, were called to be free. But do not use your freedom to indulge the flesh; rather, serve one another humbly in love.”

    Galatians 5:13 (NIV)

    Serving others is a good feeling. It’s humbling and rewarding, and it’s the gift that keeps on giving. When you see your partner stepping up to the plate and serving you well (or the other way around), that effort tends to be returned.

    Our oldest loves to bake. But she doesn’t always enjoy the thought of cleaning the kitchen after she has made some delicious baked goods. What I can tell you is that once she finishes, she is beaming with joy. There is a real sense of accomplishment in knowing you finished a task that will serve your family well.

    Something that has been stirring in my head and my heart is what kind of legacy I am going to leave. When I think about legacy, the first thing that comes to mind is the people in history who have left one: Abraham Lincoln, Mother Teresa, Gandhi, Muhammad Ali, Socrates, Nelson Mandela, Martin Luther King Jr., Jesus.

    How can I even think about living up to those names? I may never have my name in a book, I may never break a sports record, and my name will probably be forgotten in three or four generations. But what I do know is that I have a wife and children I can influence and be a role model for, and that I can pass something down to them. The reality check is this: what do you want that to be? Pride or humility? Selflessness or selfishness?

    My professional experience has also shaped the way I see household work. I once read a professional development book called Leadership and Self-Deception, by the Arbinger Institute, and it was seriously a game changer for me. It opened my eyes to a lot in the workplace, but it can just as easily be applied at home.

    To the business professionals: if you are in leadership at your company and one of your employees is struggling to progress or needs help growing, it is your job to step in and help. Whether you simply talk it through with them or provide tools and resources, you are taking the time to communicate, help resolve issues and grow their knowledge so they can move into the next step of their career.

    If you aren’t in leadership, that’s okay. But as an employee, if there is an issue at work (sales are slow, or customers are unhappy), would you try to help find a solution? Would you get the team together to brainstorm how to improve? Absolutely! Again, you would, because it’s your job.

    That is our job at home too: to brainstorm, communicate and pull together as a team.

    My wife once challenged me with these same questions. She asked, “Are you leading your family to success the way you lead your team at work? Are you being as intentional with your ‘one-on-ones’ with your kids and spouse as you are with your employees?” It’s not always easy to reflect on yourself, but I want to encourage you. If you get nothing else from this, please get this:

    Just as you have roles and responsibilities at work, you have them at home too. At work, you have a team of people you are responsible for: to encourage them, build them up and help them progress. You have the same responsibilities at home. We all understand that work is important and pays the bills, but we have other responsibilities to our families as well: a responsibility to take an active role in your family, to lead, to communicate and to help them achieve success, in whatever form you all decide.

    Stuart

    Most fights about the load are really about assumptions nobody said out loud. The fix is not keeping score. It’s deciding, ahead of time and out loud, who owns what.

    Talk about the work, not the person

    “You never do anything” starts a fight. “I need you to own school forms, start to finish” starts a plan. Talk about areas and parts of the job, using the ownership map from Chapter 2, and save it for a calm moment like the family meeting.

    Scripts you can borrow

    • “I don’t need help with this. I need you to own it, start to finish, including remembering it.”
    • “When you ask me what needs doing, I still have to do the thinking. Can you take over the noticing for     ?”
    • “I know you’ll do it differently than I would. That’s okay.”
    • “I’m not saying you don’t do anything. I’m saying I’m carrying the thinking, and I can’t keep carrying all of it.”
    • “Can we look at the ownership map together on Sunday?”

    When they won’t take part

    Most systems for splitting the load assume a willing partner. Some of you don’t have one right now, or you are parenting solo, or across two homes. This part is for you.

    Run the meeting anyway

    A weekly meeting with yourself and your kids still gets the week out of your head. Give kids areas they can own for their age.

    Lower the bar on purpose

    Decide what can go undone this season. Choosing to let it go is different from failing to get to it.

    Swap or pay for help

    A sitter swap with another family, a meal train, a cleaner once a month if you can afford it. Help you arrange still counts.

    Protect your minimums

    When you are carrying more, protecting your capacity (Chapter 7) matters more, not less.

    When agreements keep falling through

    You agreed on who owns it. Then it got dropped, again. Before you take it back, try this.

    1. Name the pattern, not the person. “School forms have been late three times this month. Can we figure out what’s getting in the way?”
    2. Check the handoff. Did they get all four parts, or just the doing? If you still do the noticing, it isn’t handed over yet.
    3. Agree on what “done” looks like. For example: signed and returned by the due date, without a reminder.
    4. Set a check-in date. Look at it together at the weekly family meeting for the next month.
    5. Shrink the area. If the whole area is too much right now, hand over a smaller piece and build up.
    6. Don’t quietly pick it back up. If you redo it every time it’s late, it never really changes hands. Let small things be late or imperfect when it’s safe to.

    If you have tried all of this for months and nothing moves, read the honest checkpoint below.

    A page for your partner

    Ch. 3 · tear out and share

    When I ask you to take something over, I’m not asking for help with a task. I’m asking you to own the thinking.

    Every part of running our family has four parts: noticing what’s needed, planning it, doing it, and following up. Right now I carry most of the noticing, planning and following up, even when you do the doing. That’s the part that wears me out, because it never turns off.

    Run Family Meetings That Actually Work

    A little planning, out loud, together.

    A family meeting moves what is in your head onto the table, where everyone can see it. It doesn’t need to be long, formal or perfect. It needs to happen most weeks. When it doesn’t, you just start again next week.

    The one thing to remember

    What is a family meeting?

    A family meeting is a regular time for the whole family to talk about anything that has to do with your family. It is a set time when everyone gets together to share, discuss, plan, strategize and carry the burden of the household together, as a team.

    When should family meetings take place?

    For family meetings to work, they have to be consistent and regular. We schedule yearly, monthly and weekly meetings for our family:

    • Yearly: on New Year’s Eve.
    • Monthly: usually on the first Sunday of the month, or the day before the month starts.
    • Weekly: every Sunday afternoon.

    We have found that New Year’s Eve and Sundays fit best, because they help you start your year and your week organized, calm and ready for what is coming. But if Sunday doesn’t work for you, that is totally okay! Look at your schedule strategically and put it on the day that makes the most sense for you and your family.

    Sunday Adelaja0

    Our personal experience

    I’ve always been a planner. Every year, I would buy a brand-new planner and make big plans to keep my family organized and cut down on the chaos. And year after year, we failed at it. We would forget to sign forms, show up at the kids’ schools on random days off and not have what they needed for school the next day. We lived in constant chaos. And I thought that was normal. After all, we have a big family. That’s just mom life, right?

    Fast-forward to today: we have more on our plate than we ever have. We live in a state where we don’t know a single person, we run our own business and we have four kids. And yet, we are more organized than ever before. Things aren’t perfect all the time, but for the most part, we run like a well-oiled machine. And it is beautiful.

    So what changed? It wasn’t a better planner. It was talking to each other. Sitting down together on a regular rhythm, out loud, to look at what was coming, decide who was handling what and make room for rest and fun. That is what a family meeting is.

    That doesn’t mean we never get pushback from the kids, or that we never skip our routines to take a day of rest. Rigidity is never a good plan with kids. But predictability and clear communication are where you will find the sweet spot of a well-functioning family team.

    We love the term “family team” because it helps everyone understand that they are an important part of the family, and that everyone has a role to play in making it work. To us, a team is a group of people with different skills and different jobs who work toward the same goal and support each other along the way.

    We want our kids to know that each of us has a role, and that those roles help our family work better together than it ever could without them. We all have feelings, interests, responsibilities, schedules and wants, and every one of those deserves to be respected and talked about clearly.

    And it shouldn’t be a one-person show. You might lead the first few meetings while everyone learns the rhythm, but after that, both parents should be actively engaged, and Dad should know how to run the meeting without you.

    By creating and protecting this sacred space for family communication, we cut down on miscommunication, missed deadlines and the chaos of busyness, and we stop assuming who is responsible for what.

    What the research says

    A 2002 review of 50 years of studies linked regular family routines with parents feeling more capable, children’s adjustment and marital satisfaction. The authors also pointed out weak methods in many of those studies, so take it as encouraging, not as proof.11

    The ground rules

    Both parents run it

    You might lead the first few meetings while everyone learns the rhythm. After that, both parents should be actively engaged, and Dad should know how to run it without you.

    Keep it short

    Fifteen minutes for the weekly meeting. Use a timer. Anything bigger goes on the monthly agenda.

    Everyone gets a voice

    Ask each person what they need this week. Kids notice more than we think. Around age 4 is a great time to start them, sometimes even younger.

    End with something fun

    Pick the movie, plan a treat, choose Saturday’s outing. People come back to meetings that end well.

    If ADHD is in your house

    Keep it visual: one whiteboard or one shared note, not five apps. Print the agenda so nobody has to remember it. Let kids move, doodle or hold something while they talk. Use a timer you can see.

    Family meeting FAQs

    How will a family meeting help you?
    A lot of times, especially in bigger families, there is just a lot going on. We used to struggle to carve out quality time for each of the kids in a way they enjoyed and needed. These meetings helped with that tremendously! It is the time when even the little ones can share what they need or want, when you can tell your spouse what your needs and stressors are, and when you can find solutions together instead of in passing.

    Who runs the meeting?
    You might lead the first few while everyone learns the rhythm. After that, both parents should be actively engaged, and Dad should know how to run the meeting without you. If only one parent ever runs it, the meeting turns into one more thing on that parent’s plate.

    What ages are appropriate?
    That’s up to you. We usually hold family meetings with our older kids, so we fit them in during nap time or after the baby goes down. Our older kids participate really well. I think around age 4 is the perfect time to start bringing them in (sometimes even younger, if they can follow what’s going on).

    How long should it take?
    Keep the weekly meeting to about 15 minutes. Anything bigger can wait for the monthly meeting.

    What if we miss a week?
    Just pick it back up the next week. A missed meeting isn’t a failed system. Use the 10-minute bad-day version if that’s all you have.

    How do we delegate and communicate chores and needs?
    We have found that transparency, talking about the family’s needs as a whole, and a whole lot of respect work best. Rather than placing blame or expecting someone else to just know what to do, talk through what the family needs, then decide together how each of you can help and serve each other.

    Why do we believe a family should work as a team?
    We believe a family functions better when everyone fully understands their roles and responsibilities, and that clear communication is the key to a well-functioning family. We also know that when the daily demands of life aren’t planned for, they start to take over, and we end up reacting instead of planning ahead. Working as a team helps everyone feel prepared, listened to and heard. It also gives everyone the chance to use their strengths and play an active role in the family. And including kids in conversations about money, chores, responsibilities, food prep and more gives them a realistic understanding of “real life” and what it takes to run a household.

    Raise Kids Who Participate

    Give kids a real part, and reward the effort.

    Kids help more when the job fits their strengths and the effort gets noticed. This chapter covers chores by strengths, a simple reward system that runs on paper “bucks” and a weekly trade-in, and a version made for kids with ADHD.

    The one thing to remember

    Important points for discussion, and how to handle chores

    Family meetings give my family a place to talk openly, not only about our schedules and needs but also about any grievances we have with each other and our frustrations with how the house is running. If responsibilities or “jobs” aren’t getting done, this is our set time to talk about it. And if someone is doing great, this is also a great time to point that out specifically.

    These team meetings have also been the biggest way I have been able to change my family’s attitude toward housework.

    When we go over our schedule and household needs, I make sure to bring up the grocery list, meal options and meal planning, and the jobs around the house that need to be done. Being that open and transparent about what needs to get done doesn’t just help your family step up. It also shows how the load is divided. If one person is carrying most of the weight, it will show.

    One way I approach household chores, like cleaning, is by having a conversation about what it takes to make our household run. You really only need to have this conversation once. After that, you can keep it going by updating “jobs” in your monthly family meetings. Keep the conversation open, with no judgment and no blame. It is simply a conversation about what the family needs and what it generally takes to run the household.

    Then you can start asking your kids, “Looking at all the jobs that need to be done around the house, which ones do you think you would be really good at?” or “Where do you feel you could serve the family best?” From there, point out their individual strengths. For example: “Brooklynn, one of your strengths is organization. You do a really great job seeing where things fit best and what makes sense. So why don’t you take charge of organizing the books in your room?” Then let them organize the books in a way that is, yes, clean, but most of all makes sense to them (because it is their room and their stuff). Let them walk away from that job with a sense of pride, belonging and importance.

    The point is to build them up, highlight their strengths and show them that their strengths are an important part of how the family thrives: without them, the job wouldn’t get done, or wouldn’t get done as well. It makes household tasks and everyday responsibilities feel less mundane and less unimportant. And it helps your family truly understand that everyone has a role to play, and that we can use our strengths to serve other people. That starts at home.

    Another benefit of approaching chores this way is that you don’t have to force them into jobs they will resent and simply hate. Constantly nagging is stressful for us, and it’s annoying for them too. No one likes to be controlled or forced into anything. Not even us! And I think we expect our children to comply with demands we would hate to have forced on us.

    My oldest daughter literally hates touching dirty dishes, but my son couldn’t care less. So he usually helps with the dishes. My son, on the other hand, hates organizing, and my daughter loves it. So she tends to help more with tidying up and organizing. We are all different, and each of us can serve the family in the areas that aren’t someone else’s strength. I think that is a great lesson for children to learn: we are all different, and we all serve a different purpose, but we can use our different strengths to reach one shared goal.

    We still expect our kids to help each other out. We still want them to know that even when something isn’t their job, there will be times they have to step up and do something they don’t like. But this approach also gives them the freedom to feel like they aren’t being controlled 100% of the time, like they have a voice, and like their feelings, words, needs and wants mean something to us. And that is important!

    Where this system came from

    After trying every sticker and chore chart on the market, we realized they were not working. Either we didn’t follow through with them, or they didn’t work for our kids. The reward charts focused only on accomplishments, and the chore charts focused only on items checked off a list. Neither felt like it supported effort, wise choices, kindness and the other positive behaviors we wanted to encourage. The red/yellow/green system that many classrooms use didn’t feel good either. It felt a little shaming, and we didn’t feel it was effective at encouraging wise choices.

    After much thought and a lot of research, we came up with this reward system, and it has been super effective and extremely encouraging with all four of our kids (even our youngest, at 18 months old!).

    Below, we outline the entire system, how it works and how to bring it into your family! We are excited to see how it helps your kiddos and takes some of the stress off the parents!

    How the Effort Bank works

    1. Make your bucks. Print the Effort Bucks at the end of this chapter, or make your own and name them after your family.
    2. Give each child an envelope. Keep a “bank” envelope with the extra bucks.
    3. Choose a trade-in day. Once a week, kids trade bucks for something on the reward menu, or save them for something bigger.
    4. Build the menu together. Kids help choose the rewards. Small, medium, big, and one-on-one time with a parent.
    5. One rule: kids can’t ask for bucks. That keeps the focus on the choice, not the payment. It also means you have to keep noticing.
    6. Earned bucks stay earned. If behavior needs a consequence, keep it separate from the bank and related to what happened (the toy goes on a shelf for the afternoon, screen time ends early). Keep it calm and short, and never in the middle of a meltdown.

    Who holds the bank? Not always mom. Let the other parent or an older child be the banker, or this becomes one more job for the default parent.

    What to reward, and how to say it

    What to encourage (not performance based)

    We do not want this reward system to be based only on performance or accomplishments. We really want to recognize their effort, their hearts, their willingness to help, their initiative and more.

    If we only praise the result or the accomplishment, they will equate their accomplishments with their worth, and that is not what we want. We also don’t want to praise them when they didn’t do something well. We don’t want to raise them with the false identity that they are the most incredible humans with no faults. We want to encourage good choices, and respond to bad ones with calm, fair consequences. Don’t reward performance alone; reward kindness, thoughtfulness and a willingness to pitch in with family chores.

    And don’t be afraid to encourage the kids to do or redo something properly, such as making their bed. It is totally acceptable to hold them to a high standard (age-appropriately, of course). If they did a crappy job, it’s okay to tell them; just do it in a nice, non-attacking way. I like to phrase it this way: “I appreciate you trying to make your bed, but I know you are capable of making it better than you did. Let me know if you would like some help, and I can show you an easier way.” This encourages their effort, lets them know they are capable of more, and lets them figure it out on their own while knowing you are there to help at any point. After a few tries, most kids will start asking for help on their own. That, in turn, shows them that you believe in them and that you are also a resource for them. It isn’t controlling at that point, because they are asking for your expertise (in bed making!). You can do this with most things, as long as it is safe: making beds, doing chores, homework and other small choices as they grow. Give them space to learn and make mistakes before you jump right in!

    One tip: make sure you aren’t correcting them every single time. Pick your battles. If you overcorrect them, you will end up discouraging them, and we don’t want to do that either!

    Here are some ways I like to encourage the kids when I give them an Effort Buck:

    • “I really loved how you helped your little sister. That was so kind of you.”
    • “Thank you for taking the initiative to pick up those toys without being asked. That was super helpful.”
    • “I appreciate your attitude and your willingness to help Mommy out.”
    • “I loved how you sensed the stress in the house and were so proactive about playing with the little girls so Mommy and Daddy could finish that phone call. That was very helpful.”
    • “Thank you for doing your after-school jobs without being asked. It is nice to see that I can trust you.”

    We want to keep affirming that we love them, we trust them and we are proud of them, and that staying on that path brings good consequences.

    What the research says

    In a classic set of studies, children praised for being smart were more likely to pick easier tasks afterward, and showed less motivation, than children praised for their effort.12

    The ADHD edition

    If your child has ADHD, the same system works better with a few changes.

    Start here: this is not treatment

    For young children with ADHD, the CDC recommends behavior therapy first, especially parent training in behavior management, and the American Academy of Pediatrics guideline does the same.15,16 This reward system is not that training.     Build Routines Around Real Life

    Routines built for the life you actually have.

    Every routine you see online quietly assumes something: a partner on duty, kids who sleep, a quiet house, money for help. This chapter helps you build routines around the help you actually have, plus three small setups that save a lot of mornings.

    If a routine needs what you don’t have, it’s the wrong routine for this season.

    The one thing to remember

    Name the hidden enabler

    Plenty of moms push back on the “wake up at 5am” advice, and for good reason.18 Before you copy anyone’s routine, ask what it quietly depends on.

    • Who is watching the kids while this happens?
    • How much sleep does it assume?
    • Does it need a quiet house?
    • Does it cost money (a gym, childcare, delivery)?
    • Does it assume a predictable job, or kids who sleep through the night?

    If you don’t have what a routine assumes, it isn’t a discipline problem. It’s the wrong routine for this season.

    Three small setups that save a lot of mornings

    The launch pad

    One spot by the door for tomorrow’s things: bags, keys, forms, shoes, the library book. CHADD includes this kind of setup in its back-to-school ideas for families with ADHD.19

    The holding box

    One container for things that don’t have a decision yet, sometimes called a doom box.20 Empty it at the weekly meeting, and give it a limit so it doesn’t turn into clutter.

    The 10-minute reset

    Set a timer. Everyone tidies one room. Stop when it rings, even if it isn’t done. Done is not the goal. Better than before is.

    Tools for these setups: the hooks, zipper pouches, school binder and visual timer I recommend are in my Home Systems & Organization collection on ShopMy.

    If ADHD is in your house

    Make routines visible: a picture checklist by the door, a timer everyone can see for the 10-minute reset, and alarms that name the action (“leave for pickup”), not just a time. Give yourself a launch pad too, not just the kids.

    Ask for support

    Support doesn’t have to come from one person. Make a short list of who can take what.

    Chapter 8 of 8

    Adjust When Life Changes

    When your season changes.

    Use this every few months, or any time life shifts. It takes about 20 minutes. Keep what works, change what doesn’t, and drop what you’ve outgrown. Needing to pivot means you are paying attention, not that you did it wrong.

    In this chapter

    • What to redo first
    • Why did it stop working?
    • The Pivot Check

    Mastering your motherhood means knowing how to adjust. This chapter is how.

    The one thing to remember

    What to redo first

    Big changes don’t mean starting over. Redo the one or two systems the change hits hardest, and leave the rest alone for now.

    A new baby

    Redo the ownership map, before the baby arrives if you can. Agree on night wake-ups at a family meeting. Lower the bar on purpose.

    A new school year

    Redo the weekly meeting agenda and the morning routine. Rewrite the reward menu with your kids.

    A job change

    Redo your week at a glance, and who owns pickups, appointments and dinner.

    A new diagnosis

    For you, your child or your partner: hand over one area to make room for appointments, and use the ADHD notes in each chapter.

    A move

    Set up the launch pad first. Keep the weekly meeting going, even if it’s short. Redo the ownership map once you’ve unpacked.

    A separation or blended family

    Decide who owns school, medical and activities across both houses. Keep one shared calendar.

    Caring for a parent

    Add their care to the ownership map as a new area. Ask for support early, and drop something before you add this.

    Summer

    Swap school items in the weekly meeting for camps and childcare. Loosen routines on purpose.

    Why did it stop working?

    Before you keep, change or drop a system, name the reason it stalled. Fix that, and try again. Stalling doesn’t mean you failed.

    • No one really owned it.
    • It was too complicated for this season.
    • We never really agreed on it.
    • We forgot to run it.
    • The season changed.
    • It worked, and we outgrew it.

    When it isn’t working

    Troubleshooting

    Every family hits snags. Find the one that sounds like yours.

    “My partner agrees, then doesn’t follow through.”

    Check that the handoff covered all four parts, agree on what “done” looks like, and set a check-in at the weekly meeting. See “When agreements keep falling through” in Chapter 3.

    “My kids refuse to take part.”

    Start smaller: one job they are good at, and one they choose. Keep the meeting short and end with something fun. Notice effort out loud. See Chapters 4 and 5.

    “I forget to run the system.”

    Tie it to something you already do, like the weekly meeting with Sunday coffee. Put the agenda and reward menu where you will see them. A missed week isn’t a failed system. See Chapter 4.

    “Our schedule changes every week.”

    Keep the weekly meeting, and plan one week at a time with My week at a glance. Fix only the anchors and must-dos. See Chapters 4 and 6.

    “I’m doing this alone.”

    Run the meeting with your kids, give older kids whole areas, and line up support outside your home. See “When they won’t take part” in Chapter 3 and “Ask for support” in Chapter 7.

    “It worked, then it stopped.”

    Find out why it stalled before you drop it. See “Why did it stop working?” and the Pivot Check in Chapter 8.
