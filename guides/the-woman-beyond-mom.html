    Who are you, outside of being Mom?

    You are a whole person, not just a role. Getting to know her again isn’t selfish.

    The one thing to remember

    What is a healthy, happy mom?

    Our definition of a healthy, happy mom is this: someone who knows herself, who can assess a situation and respond in a way that is intentional, not reactive, who can communicate clearly with those around her, and who understands herself and her emotions and loves herself.

    A woman who is emotionally healthy and emotionally intelligent, who seeks change, growth and accountability. A mother who is imperfect and okay with that, but doesn’t use it as an excuse to stay stuck. A mother who values herself, cares for herself and takes time to make sure she is healthy mentally, emotionally and physically, and who is willing to prioritize her health so she can show up well for her family. Someone who is willing to ask for help, without shame and without guilt.

    A mom who loves her family fiercely and protects them with healthy boundaries, because she is aware of her pain, her trauma and her past, but doesn’t let those things dictate her future. A mom who parents from intention, not from trauma, anger or bitterness. A woman who won’t accept the victim mentality of motherhood, and who doesn’t sacrifice herself at the altar of motherhood long-term.

    We believe moms can serve their families and themselves so much better when they are healthy. Plain and simple.

    No one functions at their best when they are tired, exhausted or rushed. That is when we are at our worst with our partners and our kids. So why would we want to function that way all the time?

    We will have bad days, and that is totally okay. But staying there isn’t good for us. It wreaks havoc on your mind, your body and your family. Through this book, my hope is to teach, inspire and equip you to be a healthy, happy mom, and that you will not only take on these healthy habits, but pass them on and pass them around to your motherhood community.

    As you work through these pages, you will start to see how to get there, and how to line up your daily decisions and habits with that goal. It is totally possible to flourish in motherhood, even with bad days mixed in. So get ready to do some work, mamas. This will be an investment, for sure. You have to be prepared to invest your mind and your time. And as with all good investments, there WILL be a return. I know you will bloom and flourish with these tools and lessons, and I can’t wait to hear how your life changes.

    Do you know who you are? (Outside of being a mama)

    When was the last time you sat alone and thought about YOU? Not in the typical “How am I going to survive one more minute?” way, but in a strategic way. When was the last time you thought about what brings you joy, or what steals your joy? Do you know what fills you up? Are you aware of what empties you? What motivates you, what drains you, what energizes you, and what makes you feel good, happy and joyful?

    What about your schedule? When was the last time you thought about how you could change it to suit you? When was the last time you looked back over the previous month and asked yourself, “How did I fill myself up last month? What choices did I make to help myself thrive in this busy, crazy season of motherhood?”

    If you are living day to day in survival mode, I get it. But at some point, you have to realize that it isn’t sustainable. Survival mode is meant to be a short season, not your entire journey as a mama.

    All too often, we give in to the crazy, overburdened, worn-out, exhausted life that supposedly “comes with motherhood.” Instead of accepting that story without question, I want you to rethink it. I firmly believe we have choices in our lives, options in our weeks and choices in our days. We can choose a different motherhood. But we can only do that when we truly know what depletes us and what gives us life. If we don’t know these fundamental truths, how will we ever know what to feed and what to prune?

    A perspective shift

    Abigail Brenner, M.D., writes that what you perceive is what you believe: your beliefs shape your attitudes, your attitudes shape how you feel, and your feelings largely determine how you behave.0 (You’ll find her full quote in Chapter 3.)

    So how do you perceive yourself? Do you believe you deserve time and resources? Or do you feel guilty when you need or want something, or when you need a break? How do you perceive self-care? Do you think it’s selfish? Do you think getting ready is a waste of time? Or do you value it, knowing it has a huge return on investment?

    When you see self-care as selfish or unimportant, you will believe you are being selfish or wasting time when you do it. Then that belief shapes your attitude. And if you have a bad attitude toward something, you won’t do it. You won’t make it a priority.

    We’ve all heard the saying, “If mama ain’t happy, ain’t nobody happy.” There is so much truth to it! Problems show up in every area of our lives. We have behavior issues with our kiddos, marriage issues, overbooked schedules and overwhelmed hearts, messy homes and too much clutter. But the outside is often a reflection of the battles within. The truth is, so much of it really does start with US.

    Have you lost yourself in motherhood?

    All too often, we dive into motherhood and marriage at full speed. It is all-consuming, and it can take up all of our time and energy. Some of that comes with the season, and some of it we can change.

    From the outside looking in, motherhood is glorified. We see those gorgeous maternity photos and newborn shoots and think: YES. I cannot wait for that. But what those pictures don’t show are the millions of doubts and questions, the adult diapers, the leaky boobs, and the endless decisions, from when to start them on real food, to which school they should go to, to “Am I ruining their lives by telling them no?” No one really tells you how hard the mental side of all this is: the weight of making nonstop decisions and wondering if you are messing your kids up.

    Meanwhile, our kids walk around the house knowing they run it, and we end up sobbing at the end of the day in our sweats, wondering where the heck the day went and how we made it all day without a shower or a real meal.

    How do you know if you’ve lost yourself?

    • You can’t remember the last thing you did just for you.
    • Your calendar is full of their activities and empty of yours.
    • You feel guilty when you rest, or when someone else watches the kids.
    • If you had a free hour, you wouldn’t know what to do with it.
    • When someone asks how you are, you answer with how the kids are.

    Can I give you a little truth bomb?

    Your kids do not have to be the center of your universe for you to be a “good mom,” or for them to turn out to be “good kids.” It might even be better for them if you stopped controlling, stopped overprotecting and let them have real human experiences (good and bad) that will shape who they become.

    We are not here to control them. We are not here only to protect them from all the bad stuff. We are not here to make every decision for them. We are here to equip them, teach them and raise them to go off on their own. One day they will leave the nest, and what will you be left with besides old sweatpants and the rude awakening of asking yourself, “Who the hell am I without these kids?”

    There is power in knowing who you are: recognizing that you are a person outside of your family, and that every one of us is an individual doing life together. Look at yourself as an individual, without guilt. Do things that bring you joy. Chase your dreams alongside your kids, and let them watch you do it. Run with them at the park and lie with them in the grass. And then know that when you leave them with a sitter or a family member, that is a good thing too.

    Too much of anything isn’t good. Too much sugar is bad, too much coffee is bad, too much bread is bad. The same goes for parenting. Too much control isn’t good, and too much freedom isn’t either. Abandoning your kids is not “self-care,” but losing yourself in motherhood can hurt everyone involved, including them.

    If any of this resonates, let me ask you a few questions. If motherhood has become all-consuming:

    • How is your marriage doing?
    • How are your mind and heart doing?
    • Do your kids understand that they are not the center of the universe?
    • Are you letting them experience how the “real world” works (within reason for their age, obviously)?

    None of us are the center of the universe. We are a teeny, teeny, small part of it. And while we can all make an impact, we are minuscule. That is a huge lesson kids can learn early, at home, or the hard way when they leave it.

    Don’t you think it’s possible to love our children fiercely while also letting them experience hurt, beauty, joy, good influences, bad influences, rules, boundaries, time with other adults, time with us, time alone with their siblings, time with friends, playground arguments, “mean kids” and kind kids? Isn’t that what we all deal with as adults?

    The world is not bubble-wrapped, and it is not full of 100% kind people. It has hurt and pain, beauty and joy, fights and making up, and people who think differently than we do. We all have rules and laws to follow, and boundaries to respect. The best way to keep our kids from being completely shocked by the real world is to safely let them experience it as they grow up.

    So yes, they will be fine when you shower alone. They will be fine when you put on a movie so you can eat the delicious, nutritious salad you just ordered from Uber Eats in peace and quiet. They will be fine when you drop them at a friend’s house so you can go on a date. And your partner will be fine when you leave them with all the kids so you can get a pedicure, read a book or, heck, finally write that book you’ve always wanted to write!

    Love your babies fiercely. But love yourself fiercely enough to remember that they are only in your home for a short time. One day they will be out in the real world, and so will you. Find a balance that makes room for both of you. If you are crying at the end of every day, something is off, friends. If every day feels like Groundhog Day, something is off. Make changes.

    I say this all the time: you are the gatekeeper of your home. You are the curator of your life. You are raising the humans who will shape our next generation. You have power in your daily choices, in what you allow and in the standards you set for your home. You are not a victim of motherhood; you are living it alongside your kids. Use the power you have in your choices to benefit everyone, LONG TERM.

    The one thing to remember

    What depletes you?

    Depletion means something is being used up faster than it is filled back up.

    Oftentimes, we feel depleted because of all our daily duties, maybe our circumstances, and often simply the season we are in. But a lot of the time, it is also because of decisions we make throughout the day without realizing it. Maybe even some we make on purpose. We aren’t aware of what depletes us, and we don’t leave time to fill ourselves back up.

    Personally, one thing that consistently depletes me is too much socializing. As much as I crave community and friendship, it is the one thing that completely empties me. I struggle with constant conversation, small talk, big social gatherings, my phone going off all the time, and so on. My friendships are limited to a few close ladies, and that’s it. I struggle when family is in town, not because I don’t want to see them, but because it is usually nonstop for that week, that weekend, two weeks or however long they are in town. Being fully immersed in socializing is too intense for me. I used to feel really bad about this. I used to say yes out of obligation, then come home feeling beyond empty, with nothing left to give my family.

    But when I realized that this specific thing doesn’t fill me up, and actually does the opposite, I had to make a decision to stop feeling guilty and stop saying yes to things I felt obligated to say yes to. Once I accepted this truth about me, life got so much lighter. A straightforward choice made my mind feel more at peace, lowered my stress and removed so much of the guilt I was carrying every single day.

    Another thing on my list that completely wears me down is a dirty house. I hate waking up to a mess, and I hate clutter. One day I woke up and realized I couldn’t live this way anymore. So I got rid of a ton of crap, minimized, and realized we needed to make daily choices to help keep the clutter down. We needed to get on the same page as a family and put better systems and rhythms in place to support the home we wanted to live in.

    One thing to recognize: the thing that depletes you might actually fill someone else up, and vice versa. That is the beauty of getting to know yourself. You can make proactive, informed decisions that are best for you and your family, without the guilt. Comparing yourself to someone else doesn’t do much for you, other than steal your joy and rob you of living in your truth.

    What sustains you?

    Sustenance is what keeps you going: whatever maintains you and keeps you alive and well.

    Now that we know the difference between sustenance and depletion, and that we can actually choose between the two, we can focus on how to fill ourselves back up.

    First, we need to understand that we are always trading something. We trade time or money for something. Everything comes with a price, and some things are worth sacrificing time or money for. Once we understand that, we can really start to commit to filling ourselves back up, knowing up front what the sacrifice will be.

    So, what sustains you? Do you need quiet time each morning to get your mind and body ready? That might mean sacrificing some sleep, but is it worth it to you to start your day filled up?

    Do you love to work out, but you have 27 kids? What can you do at home? Can you join a gym and use the childcare? Do you love getting ready? Do you enjoy wearing a cute outfit?

    Really think through what sustains you and makes you feel good.

    One thing that fills me up is quiet time. Not necessarily first thing in the morning, but I do need quiet time without my kids and without my husband. This downtime lets me decompress, process what happened that day or what I need to do, and reset myself. Alone time can be hard to come by, but I have to make it a priority.

    With a big family, it feels like my husband and I barely have any alone time as it is, so taking time away from my kids and our marriage seems pretty selfish, right? I start to feel guilty when I do it. I feel guilty when I ask for it. But once I actually take the alone time, I feel SO much better. I feel more at peace and calmer, I am more patient with my husband and kids, I handle stress better, and overall I feel like I can manage as a mom better. I have to sacrifice time with my family to be a better mom. It sounds counterintuitive, but it works.

    Being a better mom and wife does not always mean being with your family 24/7. Sometimes it means stepping away and thinking about yourself for a small moment in your day or your week, so that you can come back to them with love, patience and joy.

    Work through the guilt of having your own needs and wants, and accept that you are a human outside of your children and your family. Sometimes you might actually have to explain this to your kids and your partner.

    There are crazy days with a big family, and I find that sitting down with my older kids and reminding them that I am human (I get hungry and tired too, I get angry and frustrated, and I want and need things too) helps them understand and behave a little better.

    The Enneagram

    What is the Enneagram, and why is it relevant?

    At first, I was extremely resistant to the Enneagram, and I knew nothing about it. I typically dislike trends, and I assumed it was just the next one to come and go, and that it wasn’t significant. Boy, was I wrong.

    The Narrative Enneagram, a teaching organization, calls it a powerful tool for personal and collective transformation, and explains the name this way: “Stemming from the Greek words ‘ennea’ (nine) and ‘grammos’ (a written symbol), the nine-pointed Enneagram symbol represents nine distinct strategies for relating to the self, others and the world.”0

    Most people don’t like personality tests because they don’t want the results to put them in a box of limitations. I urge you to set that thought aside and take the test anyway. That is the last thing that will happen when you take this test and get your results!

    I’m going to have you take a free online test to get a better idea of what personality type you are. But I would highly encourage you to get the book The Road Back to You, by Ian Morgan Cron and Suzanne Stabile.0 Early in the book, the authors make a point that stopped me in my tracks: most of us assume we understand who we are when we don’t. We don’t question the lens we see the world through: where it came from, how it has shaped our lives, or whether what it shows us is distorted or true. Most of us aren’t aware of how the things that helped us survive as kids are now holding us back as adults. And what we don’t know about ourselves can hurt us, and the people around us.

    How true is that? How long have we been living on autopilot, unaware of why we do the things we do, or doing things out of obligation, pressure or comparison? Aren’t you tired of it? The first step is figuring out who the heck YOU are, my friend.

    Once you take the test, give yourself some time to digest the results before you explore how they affect you, your parenting and your relationships. I didn’t expect the process of “digestion” to take as long as it did. It really took me some time to absorb my results and how they fit with the way I saw myself before the test. Now that I’ve done the test and fully understand the results, I am so freaking thankful. It has given me so much insight into not only how I function, but how I can improve my decisions and behaviors.

    What the research says

    The Enneagram is popular, but the research behind it is limited. A 2021 review of 104 samples found mixed evidence that Enneagram tests are reliable and valid, and little support for some of its finer points.0 It isn’t a clinical or diagnostic tool. Use it as a lens for reflection, not a label.

    A word of encouragement before you take the test

    When you take the test, you will find out some things about yourself that you probably won’t like. I was pretty upset when I first saw my type. But here is one thing that helped me get past it: a line from the writer Flannery O’Connor, quoted in The Road Back to You, that the first product of self-knowledge is humility.

    The one thing to remember

    What is mindset, and why does it matter?

    Your mindset is the set of core beliefs you hold about yourself and the world.

    It determines how you feel, what actions you take and the habits you build. Your mindset shapes who you are and how you respond to what happens in your life. Your life right now (your level of success, your emotional state, your self-esteem, your income and how happy you are) reflects a lot of things, including some you can’t control, like money, health, support and the culture we live in. But it also reflects your mindset, your habits and your daily choices, and those are the parts you get to work on.

    It is easy to slip into a victim mentality without realizing it. Most of us don’t know we have it unless someone points it out or we do some serious soul searching.

    By a victim mentality, I mean a mindset where life always feels like it is happening to you, and someone or something else is always to blame. The part to watch for is what it lets us avoid: taking responsibility for our own part.

    Do you ever have conversations wrapped in excuses, simply wanting someone to relate to you, to agree that motherhood is hard and to empathize with you? We do need empathy sometimes. But we also have to realize that empathy won’t change the fact that we have to wake up again tomorrow morning and do the same thing all over again.

    Intentional, simplified habits are what we need.

    I urge you: don’t play the victim in your own life or circumstances. It doesn’t work. And don’t wear busyness as a badge of honor. Busyness is not a good thing. Busyness is a distraction. The thoughts and turmoil will still be there when your schedule calms down if you don’t deal with the source.

    We have to understand that we are limited human beings who can only put a certain amount of energy toward a certain number of things each day. Our energy is limited. That means we need to make wise choices every day, even down to the thoughts we think. Our thoughts take energy, and our thoughts shape our lives.

    Think of it this way: every thought you have is either pushing you toward your goals or pulling you further away from them. Are your thoughts positive, affirming and encouraging, or discouraging and downright unkind?

    How you speak to yourself, and the thoughts you think, matter more than you might believe.

    Maybe this isn’t something at the front of your mind. If so, that just means there is room to grow here. Your own voice is so familiar that it doesn’t occur to most people that they can change it. But you can flip the script on the conversations you have with yourself and reshape your thoughts.

    I’ve found that low, heavy seasons get harder when I stop setting myself up for success. By honoring our bodies and our minds in simple ways, we can create a massive ripple effect in our kids, our marriages and our communities. (Depression itself is a real health condition, not a mindset problem.0 If you think you might be depressed, please talk to your doctor or a licensed therapist.)

    Here are a few takeaways I want you to walk away with:

    • Mindset matters in everything you do.
    • Consistency is key, not perfection.
    • You have more control over your life, good and bad, than you give yourself credit for.
    • You have the power to do a lot of amazing things. But it’s up to you to choose to do them.

    Hold both truths

    If you only hear “it’s the system,” you can end up feeling stuck. If you only hear “it’s your mindset,” you end up blaming yourself for things no amount of positive thinking can fix, like the cost of childcare or a job with no flexibility. So this book holds both: name what isn’t yours to carry, then change what is.

    Choices

    Mastering your mindset is hard. And it is a continual process, not something you master once and are done with. But here is what I want you to understand: you have choices.

    You have choices in how you raise your family. You have choices in who financially provides for your household. You have choices in what school your kids go to, what environment they are in, what they watch, what they see, what they do and what influences they are around. You have choices in the food you eat. You have choices in how much you move each day. You have choices. That doesn’t mean you control every part of your life, but you do have choices.

    I could go on and on, but the bottom line is this: we have choices, whether we believe it or not. And how you use them starts with your mindset.

    Fixed vs. growth mindset

    Psychologist Carol Dweck describes two main mindsets: a fixed mindset and a growth mindset.0 Here is the difference between the two.

    In a fixed mindset, people believe their qualities are fixed traits that cannot change. They spend their time proving their intelligence and talents instead of working to develop and improve them. They also believe that talent alone leads to success, and that effort isn’t required.

    In a growth mindset, people believe their learning and intelligence can grow with time and experience. When people believe they can get smarter, they realize that their effort affects their success. So they put in the extra time, and that leads to higher achievement.

    Have you ever thought about your mindset and how you process your thoughts and decisions? Or did you not realize there were different mindsets to choose from?

    • “I’m just not good at this.”
    • A mistake means I should give up.
    • Other people’s opinions decide my worth.
    • Criticism is an attack.
    • I am who I am.

    Growth mindset

    • “I’m not good at this yet.”
    • A mistake shows me what to try next.
    • Opinions are opinions, not facts.
    • Feedback can hold some wisdom.
    • I can grow and change at my own pace.

    Moving to a growth mindset

    If you have been stuck in a fixed mindset without realizing it, I’ve got a few tips to help you move to a growth mindset.

    1. Embrace both your strengths and your weaknesses.

    When you really understand and accept both your strengths and your weaknesses, you can move forward in a proactive way. Instead of beating yourself up for what you are not good at, you remember that you are human. You are good at some things and not so good at others, just like everyone else. That doesn’t mean you can’t get better. It just means your strengths and weaknesses become facts you can plan around, not proof that something is wrong with you.

    For example, if you are not great at waking up early to “start your day off right,” then don’t force it just yet. Build your day around the time when YOU have the most energy and feel most focused and motivated. That might sound like: “While I might not be super productive at 5am like I want to be one day, I know right now that at 8pm I get an instant boost of energy and creativity once the kids are down for bed.” Then put your most important work in that window.

    When you stop fighting your weaknesses and start planning around them, you can move forward in a productive, proactive way that fits the season you are in. And when your season changes, your strengths and weaknesses may change with it. That is okay too.

    2. Challenges and “failures” aren’t bad. They are opportunities to learn and grow.

    I know so many women who are paralyzed by the fear of failing or falling short. Guess what? We are human. We are imperfect, and we are limited. Failing and falling short are simply part of being human. But they don’t have to be a negative part of life. The only power failure has is your perception of it and how it affects your mind. Instead of calling a situation that didn’t go as planned a failure, swap your thinking to: “Well, that didn’t work that way, but maybe I can rethink it, change my approach and try again.” A limited or fixed mindset will tell you that if you didn’t get it right the first time, you aren’t good at it and you should give up. That is a lie. Don’t believe it. Get creative, move past those negative feelings and fight for the things that are worth the effort.

    3. Seek to learn, not to gain approval.

    No matter what you do, someone will have an opinion about it. But here is the kicker: it is just an opinion, not a fact. Did you get that? Other people’s opinions of you are just opinions. They are not facts.

    Here is one more tip. Essentially, everything is a learning opportunity. And while I don’t seek approval, I do seek wise counsel. Seeking approval means talking through your next step or decision with someone and wanting them to approve of it, and then feeling discouraged when they don’t. Seeking wise counsel means having a few mentor-type relationships with people who know your life, your circumstances, your family, your personality, your faith and your goals for the future. They can speak life into you, and they may be able to help you see things more clearly. Wise counsel is always a great idea.

    One thing I have noticed from being in many mentor relationships is that the conversations are never really about whether they approve or disapprove. They are about asking and talking through hard questions related to that specific goal or idea. They are thought-provoking, sometimes hard and sometimes super encouraging, but the end result is always you working toward a goal with the help of some wise support.

    4. Learn to give and accept constructive criticism, and know the difference between helpful and hurtful.

    There is a lot of talk about “mommy shaming” these days, and it’s real. Being judged or torn down for how you mother hurts, and no one deserves that. But not every piece of feedback is shaming. Constructive criticism is meant to help you grow. Shaming is meant to make you feel small. Learning to tell the difference is a big part of having a growth mindset.

    When a comment stings, pause before you decide what it was, and ask yourself why it hit so hard. Sometimes it really was hurtful. But sometimes it touched something you are already sensitive about: an area where you feel insecure, an old label, or something you have been beating yourself up over. Noticing why you feel triggered doesn’t mean the other person was right. It just helps you respond to what was actually said, not to the old hurt underneath it. And if it was only meant to shame you? You can let it go.

    On the other side, I don’t think we should go around criticizing people. But I do believe that if you see something, you should say something. Sometimes it really is helpful, and staying quiet isn’t always the kind thing to do. Before you say it, ask yourself: Will this actually help her? Am I saying it to help, or to prove a point? Is this the right time and place? Do we have the kind of relationship where she can hear it from me? Then share it openly, honestly and kindly, the way you would want someone to share it with you.

    That is the wisdom here: knowing when something is helpful and when it is hurtful, whether you are giving it or receiving it. Remember that the mamas around you are doing the same thing you are, winging it every day and figuring this life out one day at a time. So lower that wall of protection a little. Have hard conversations, and accept hard conversations. You will have a surplus of advice once you become a mom. You don’t have to take all of it, but don’t shoot it all down either. Create a tribe, let your tribe come to each other honestly, and do this mom thing together.

    5. You and your mind can change over time.

    Just because you don’t know something right now doesn’t mean you never will. Just because you haven’t learned a skill yet doesn’t mean you can’t. And just because you didn’t know better before doesn’t mean you can’t do better from here on. You have the ability to grow and change at whatever pace you decide. Sometimes it takes a while, and sometimes the lessons are instant. Whatever the timeline, change is not only possible; it should be a goal. In my opinion, growth should never stop.

    6. Learning styles are different and shouldn’t be compared.

    We all learn and grow differently, so don’t compare yourself to anyone else. Keep your eyes up, look around and learn from the people around you, but don’t make yourself feel bad for being different. Give yourself the freedom to change at your own pace, in a way that works for you and serves you well.

    7. Reflect often on your progress and your willingness to learn and grow.

    Self-reflection is vital to growth. If you have a hard time looking at yourself with honest eyes, find a mentor who can reflect with you. Look at how you are doing, how you are growing, what is changing and how you are improving. Notice which areas still need work, and ask for help.

    Emotions and trauma

    A big passion of mine in recent years has been learning all about trauma: what it is and how it shows up later in life. This topic has affected every single area of my life, for most of my life, without me even being aware of it.

    How to prevent and overcome burnout

    Burnout is often predictable, and preventable to a degree.

    What the research says

    The World Health Organization describes burnout as a syndrome that comes from chronic workplace stress that hasn’t been successfully managed. It lists three signs: feeling exhausted or drained of energy, feeling more mentally distant from or negative about your job, and getting less done. The WHO uses the word only for work, not for other areas of life.0

    Researchers have since studied the same kind of exhaustion in parents. They call it parental burnout: being exhausted by your role as a parent, feeling fed up with it, feeling like you are no longer the parent you used to be, and pulling away emotionally from your kids.0

    In real life, it looks like this: you are exhausted in every way, you can’t keep up with what’s being asked of you, and the things you used to care about start to feel like too much.

    Eventually, you may feel like you have nothing left to give. (Have you ever said this to your partner or your friends: “I literally have nothing left. I am exhausted!”?)

    The negative effects of burnout spill over into every area of life, including your home life, your work life, your relationships, your marriage, your parenting and your social life. Because the consequences are so big, it’s important to deal with burnout right away.

    What you need first

While we might want to avoid stress altogether, we simply can’t. But what we can do is find healthy ways to process our emotions and stress, learn about stress, notice how our bodies respond to it and learn how to complete the stress cycle, so we don’t stay stuck. So we finally wake up to how vital self-care is, and how to make it consistent.

    So what actually happens inside your body during stress?

    Your autonomic nervous system

    You have probably heard of the fight-or-flight response. To break it down a little more, your autonomic nervous system, the part that runs things like your heartbeat and digestion without you thinking about it, has two main sides that balance each other.0

    Sympathetic: fight or flight

    • Gets your body ready for a perceived threat
    • Speeds you up: your heart beats faster, your body tenses and you become more alert
    • Slows down what isn’t needed to survive the moment, like digestion

    Parasympathetic: rest and digest

    • Brings your body back to calm after the threat passes
    • Slows your heart rate back down
    • Keeps everyday processes like digestion running when you feel safe and relaxed

    For humans and other mammals, going back to the caveman days, this survival system let us react quickly to life-threatening situations, like running from a predator. In today’s world, we may not be running from lions, but we are dealing with situations that threaten our peace of mind, our trust, our safety, our financial security and more. In our minds, it all registers as a perceived threat. And when we perceive a threat, our body has a physical response.

    When you are stressed, your body is running on your sympathetic nervous system. It is in survival mode.

    What this means is that when your mind or body senses a threat, the parts of your brain that handle complex problem solving take a back seat. Your brain switches into survival mode, and instead of well-thought-out responses and long-term thinking, it wires you for instinctive, reactive responses. On top of that, your body tells the systems that aren’t needed for survival, like digestion, to slow way down.

    Your entire body and mind change in response to the perceived threat or stressor. From there, you go into fight or flight, or you shut down completely.

    In fight mode, you might feel irritated, annoyed or angry. In flight mode, you might feel anxious, unsure, worried, scared or terrified. And there is one more mode that is rarely talked about when we discuss the nervous system and how we respond to stress: freeze.0 That’s when you shut down. You feel disconnected, paralyzed, numb, disengaged, sluggish, defeated, exhausted, empty or worn down.

    John and Julie Gottman, who study couples and relationships, call this “flooding”: when conflict or stress sends your body into that fight-or-flight state.0 They focus on relationships, but it’s the same stress response in your body.

    Staying in fight, flight or freeze mode long-term isn’t good for your mind, your body or your relationships.0

    How to complete the stress cycle

    So what do we do with stress? If stress causes this response in our bodies, how do we fix it or avoid it?

    Burnout has many causes, but one of them is getting stuck in a negative emotion and never completing the stress cycle. In their book Burnout, Emily Nagoski and Amelia Nagoski describe emotions as tunnels: if you go all the way through, you come out into the light at the end. Exhaustion happens when we get stuck partway through.0

    Most of us, in the stress cycle, get stuck in the revved-up, activated part. We go back and forth from there, but we rarely get all the way to calming down and recovering. A lot of times, we choose ways to escape. They help us feel less stressed (or less emotionally charged) in the moment, but we never actually recover or deal with the stressor. So the back and forth continues, and it wreaks havoc on our bodies and minds.

    When this happens every day, our stress response stays switched on. Keeping our bodies stuck in this cycle without completing it can lead to long-term health issues and burnout.0

    Some of the ways we exit the stress cycle too soon are escaping through shopping, drinking (wine at night), Netflix and so on. Sometimes escaping feels easier than facing the facts. Or maybe we just don’t know how, or we don’t have the energy to deal with the same issues over and over again.

    Or maybe there is no way to “solve” the issue, like a troubled marriage, issues with your kids, or issues with your parents or in-laws. So your only option feels like escaping, or numbing the pain, the anxiety, the hurt and the stress.

    But I want to challenge you to find healthy, proactive ways to complete the stress response cycle, even when you can’t control or fix the stressor itself.

    To move through the entire stress cycle, we have to identify the stressor, respond to it or resolve it, calm down from it, and then complete the cycle by being restored, or restoring ourselves. That process lets your body know the “perceived threat” is no longer a threat, and that you are safe. It brings back your parasympathetic nervous system, so your body can rest again. Your heart rate calms down, your muscles relax, your digestion gets back to work, your brain isn’t in panic mode, and you can make wiser, more long-term decisions.

    This is where real self-care comes in.

    So how do you complete the cycle? How do you go from stressed out to restored? The Nagoskis separate it into two parts: dealing with the stress, and dealing with the stressor.0 Both are important.

    First, deal with the stress

    The stress is what’s actually going on inside your body. Here are the ways the Nagoskis describe in Burnout to complete the stress response cycle, with my notes.0

    Physical activity

    We might not always want to hop on a treadmill or lift some weights, but the Nagoskis call physical activity the most efficient way to complete the stress cycle. And there is almost always a sense of accomplishment afterward.

    Deep breathing

    Slow, deep breathing helps calm your body. It anchors you to where you are right now and lets your mind and body settle. It can also help you put things into perspective, depending on the conversation you have with yourself!

    There is power in community

    A lot of moms feel isolated, and a lot of us are isolated. Time with your “safe space” friends gives your mind and body the chance to shift back into rest mode.

    Crying

    This one is so underestimated. In the middle of hard things, we think it will be like this forever, and the weight feels SO heavy. But emotions are tunnels, and there is a light at the end. Crying is one way to walk through them. It releases what has built up in your body and gives it a chance to restore itself.

    Affection

    Affection with someone you feel safe with can help your body feel like everything is okay in the world. The Nagoskis suggest a 20-second hug, or the six-second kiss that relationship researcher John Gottman recommends.

    Creative expression

    I believe everyone has some kind of creativity inside them. A lot of the time, moms abandon their creative sides, maybe because we think we don’t have time anymore, or we don’t make it a priority. But using creative expression as a regular stress reliever is a game changer!

    Notice that none of the items on that list were shopping, spending money, treating yourself to sweets or drinking wine. Those things won’t help you complete the stress response cycle. They are ways to escape and consume. That doesn’t mean they have no place in your day or week. They certainly might. But again, we have to ask ourselves: am I being proactive to create long-term change? Or am I escaping and numbing myself? And if so, what am I numbing myself from?

    If ADHD is in your house

    Stress can hit fast and hard. Pick one tool from this page and practice it on a calm day, so it’s already there when you need it. A walk around the block counts.

    Then, deal with the stressor

    The second part is dealing with the stressor. Both parts are important. But if you are at the point of burnout, I recommend dealing with the stress inside your body first. Sometimes you have to deal with the stress so you can be well enough to deal with the stressor.

    Responding to the stressor doesn’t always mean resolving it, either. There are stressors you can control and stressors you can’t. For the ones we can control, we have to be proactive. That might look like meal planning, good time management, asking for help, or delegating tasks at work and at home.

    One thing I am super passionate about is honoring the life we’ve been given. It frustrates me when “hot mess mom” gets treated like a badge of honor. Yes, we have crappy days. Yes, we have survival seasons. The postpartum season is HARD and exhausting, and it is a total survival season because of all the changes and healing that have to take place. But we shouldn’t have to stay there.

    If you notice that you are stressed every single day at 5:30 because you still don’t know what you’re making for dinner, that’s something you can change, friend. Investing an hour or two over the weekend to organize and plan your meals is so helpful and proactive! It helps you avoid that daily stress and all the unnecessary last-minute decisions that wear us down.

    It is so easy to sit in the chaos and think, “This is just motherhood.” But a lot of the chaos isn’t motherhood itself. Some of it we can change.

    But what about the stressors you can’t control: the things that are done to you, that you have to deal with often, or that just won’t go away?

    You change your perspective. Instead of fearing failure, mess-ups and hard situations, look at them as opportunities to learn. These failures and mistakes are your gym. They are the workouts that build your muscles of creativity, ingenuity and grit. They are your stepping stones to truly thriving, because every single experience is an opportunity to learn and grow. But you have to have that perspective in order to learn and grow.

    Second, we fall for what I call the myth of martyrdom. I don’t mean the dramatic, historical kind of martyr. I mean someone who willingly suffers because they think other things are more important. (AKA, a mom.) Here are some common beliefs (lies, actually) that so many adults hold today:

    • You believe that your roles as a business owner, a leader, a partner, a parent or a mother outweigh your own needs.
    • You believe you don’t have time to take care of yourself.
    • You believe everything else is more pressing or more important than sleep, eating, rest or fun.
    • You believe that taking care of yourself means you’re selfish.
    • You believe that breaks, vacations and time away aren’t productive and will hurt your work or your business.

    In my experience, the people who fall for these lies most often are parents, entrepreneurs and women. So if you are a mom who runs a business, you’re essentially screwed, unless you understand the depth and importance of boundaries, self-care and healthy habits.

Runners have to stay hydrated to finish the race.

Running yourself into the ground will. Self-care, rest and breaks give you a healthy pace, so you can keep going and not burn out.

    Why your emotions matter

    Numbing: hurry, busyness and escape

    Escapism means avoiding the unpleasant, boring, hard or scary parts of daily life by looking for distraction or relief, often through entertainment or fantasy. People sometimes use it to get relief from ongoing feelings of sadness, too.

    Escape is a coping mechanism that so many of us women and moms have normalized as “typical mom life.” We deal with the same things day in and day out, we focus on the problems, and then we numb with wine and escape with Netflix at the end of the night. I am guilty of these things sometimes, too, but I have found there can be a balance of both. There are definitely healthy amounts of escape. Everyone needs them. But when is escape too much? And how can you tell whether you are escaping in a healthy way or an unhealthy way?

    The fact is, motherhood is repetitive. We get up every day, do the same things and deal with the same issues over and over again. That is a recipe for burnout. But like I said earlier, burnout is often predictable, and preventable to a degree.

    For example, a vacation is a healthy escape when it is planned in advance and within your budget. A weekend away is a healthy escape, too, when it is planned and within your budget.

    Reading a novel or watching a movie is a way to escape. But think about your mindset when you feel the need to escape, and ask yourself the questions on the next page.

    We do the same thing with being busy and overloading our schedules. We often fill our schedules to numb, escape and avoid so many things: the problems in our marriage, the chaos in our homes, the lack of contentment we feel, the disappointment we experience with family members. Author John Ortberg has written that hurry is not just a disordered schedule; hurry is a disordered heart.0 I would even swap the word hurry for busy. Busy is not just a full, disordered schedule. It can be a discontented, disordered heart.

    So instead, I say: schedule it! Tell yourself, “I am going to be negative today. I am going to whine, bitch, moan, cry, yell, blast screamo music, whatever I need. I am going to be a whiny little bitch today. I am going to throw a fit and hate everything, because I can, and because my feelings are valid.”

    And at the end of the day, I am done with it. Tomorrow is a new day, when I put my big girl pants on and get shit done.

    For me, it works almost every time. Usually, once I have given myself permission, I find I don’t even want to complain all day. I end up getting things done anyway, and I feel so much better.

    And if one day isn’t enough, that’s okay too. Some feelings need more time: a walk, a good cry, journaling, a talk with a friend or time with a therapist.

    So give yourself some space to whine and throw a fit!

    What the research says

    In a study that followed 729 adults for 12 years, people who tended to bottle up their emotions had a higher risk of dying during that time, including from cancer.0 It was an observational study, so it shows a link, not proof that holding feelings in causes illness. It didn’t test scheduling a bad day. It just suggests that stuffing feelings down over the years isn’t harmless.

    When a bad day turns into bad weeks

    A scheduled bad day is for normal hard days. If your bad days are stretching into weeks, or you feel hopeless, that is more than a bad day. Talk to your doctor or a mental health professional. If you are in crisis, call or text 988.

    Ways to reset your mind

    We all have bad days. That doesn’t mean you are failing. It means you are having a bad day, or a bad moment. So here are some quick, effective tips and tools to help you get out of that funk and into a healthier mental space.

    • Get out of bed, and then make your bed
    • Get yourself ready
    • Give yourself 10 minutes of quiet time to calm your mind
    • Read a book
    • Call a mentor
    • Listen to a podcast
    • Go for a walk outside, or exercise
    • Write down new truth statements to shift your mind away from the negativity
    • Use your affirmations to push the negativity out
    • Journal to get your thoughts out of your head and onto paper
    • Cry to get the emotions out, and then start back at the top

It might be the season you’re in. It might be ADHD. It might be your hormones. Often it’s more than one. This chapter won’t diagnose you. It will help you notice patterns, so you can have a better conversation with someone who can.

    The one thing to remember

    Why the question is so hard to answer

    Motherhood runs on planning, remembering and keeping track of a hundred moving pieces. Those are the same everyday skills many women with ADHD describe struggling with. So when things start slipping after kids, it is genuinely hard to tell whether you are overloaded, whether you have ADHD, or both. It is such a common question that whole essays carry it as their title.4 And for many women, hormones are part of the picture too. We’ll get to those in a few pages.

    What the research says

    • A study of 363,904 mothers in Denmark’s national registers found that ADHD diagnoses dropped during pregnancy, stayed lower for about two years, then rose above pre-pregnancy levels two to five years after birth. The study cannot say why.1
    • In a CDC survey of US adults with a current ADHD diagnosis, 61% of women were diagnosed as adults, compared with 40% of men.2
    • When a clinician evaluates an adult for ADHD, part of the process is working out whether symptoms were present before age 12, even if nobody noticed them back then.3

    What research cannot tell you is whether this is ADHD for you. Only an evaluation can.

    If you have read Master Your Motherhood, you may remember my planner story from its family meetings chapter: a new planner every year, forgotten forms, showing up on random days off, and the thought that it was “just mom life.” Maybe it was. Maybe it was more. That is what this chapter is for.

    Both can be true

    You can have ADHD and also be carrying too much. A diagnosis does not make an unfair load fair, and an unfair load does not rule out ADHD. If you are evaluated and it isn’t ADHD, the overwhelm is still real, and the rest of this book is still for you.

    If your child was diagnosed first

    Some mothers first recognize themselves while learning about their child’s ADHD. One mother writing in CHADD’s Attention magazine describes seeing it in herself after several of her children were diagnosed.6 ADHD often runs in families: twin studies put its heritability at around 74%, which describes populations, not your personal odds.5 If you recognized yourself, write down what you saw. It is useful for your own appointment, and missing it in yourself does not make you a bad parent.

    Where hormones come in

    Hormones shift across your cycle, after a baby and in perimenopause. Many women say their focus, mood and patience shift with them. The research on hormones and ADHD is real but small, and it’s still early. Here is what we know, and what we don’t.

    Your cycle

    • A 2026 study that followed 30 women day by day found their ADHD symptoms were worst during their period.0
    • A review of the research describes changes in attention and focus in the second half of the cycle and in the days before a period.0 Researchers think estrogen may affect how dopamine works in the brain, which could help explain why. That idea is still being studied.

    Both rest on small numbers of women, so treat them as a reason to track your own pattern, not as a rule.

    PMDD

    PMDD (premenstrual dysphoric disorder) is a more serious form of PMS that mostly affects mood. There is a full page on it, including how it overlaps with ADHD, right after the cycle guide.0

    Write it down

    If you feel like a different person the week before your period, track it. Patterns on paper are much harder for anyone to brush off, including you.

    Your cycle, week by week

    Here is what is happening in your body across a typical cycle, and what you might notice. The weeks below use a 28-day cycle as an example. Yours may be shorter or longer, and ovulation can shift from month to month, so count from the first day of your period and adjust.0

    Week 1Your period (days 1 to about 5)

    What’s happening: estrogen and progesterone are at their lowest, and the lining of your uterus sheds.0

    What you might notice: tiredness, cramps, lower energy. In the 2026 study above, this is when ADHD symptoms were worst.0

    Try: bad-day versions of your systems, easy dinners and an early bedtime.

    Week 2Follicular phase (about days 6 to 13)

    What’s happening: estrogen climbs as your body gets an egg ready and rebuilds the uterine lining.0

    What you might notice: many people feel their energy and focus pick up as estrogen rises.

    Try: putting big projects, hard conversations and planning in this window.

    Mid-cycleOvulation (around day 14)

    What’s happening: a surge of luteinizing hormone (LH) tells an ovary to release an egg.0

    What you might notice: some people feel at their most social and energetic around now.

    Week 3Early luteal phase (about days 15 to 21)

    What’s happening: after ovulation, the follicle that released the egg becomes the corpus luteum and makes progesterone to prepare for a possible pregnancy.0

    What you might notice: some people feel calmer or more tired as progesterone rises.

    Week 4Late luteal phase (about days 22 to 28)

    What’s happening: if there is no pregnancy, the corpus luteum breaks down, estrogen and progesterone drop, and that drop starts your next period.0

    What you might notice: PMS symptoms like irritability, bloating and low mood, and for some women with ADHD, harder focus in the days before a period.0

    Try: lowering the bar on purpose, protecting sleep and planning fewer commitments.

    More about PMDD

    PMDD (premenstrual dysphoric disorder) is not “bad PMS.” It is a health condition that can take over the week or two before your period, then lift a few days after it starts.0 Estimates suggest it affects roughly 1 to 5 out of every 100 women who menstruate.0

    What it can feel like

    • Irritability or anger that feels out of proportion
    • Sadness, hopelessness or crying spells
    • Anxiety, tension or feeling on edge
    • Big mood swings, or feeling out of control
    • Trouble concentrating
    • Exhaustion, sleep changes and food cravings
    • Physical symptoms like bloating, breast tenderness and headaches

    What helps

    • Tracking your symptoms for at least two cycles. A symptom calendar helps a doctor make the diagnosis.0
    • Talking to your doctor about treatment. A family-medicine review calls certain antidepressants (SSRIs) a first-line option for severe symptoms, and there is some evidence for cognitive behavioral therapy.0 Your doctor can also talk through other options, including hormonal ones.
    • Planning your lightest weeks around it, using the tracker in this chapter.

    PMDD and ADHD

    PMDD and ADHD can look alike, and they can show up together. Both can bring trouble focusing, irritability and big emotional swings, which is why tracking matters: it shows whether your hardest days line up with your cycle.

    What the research says

    • In a 2025 UK study of more than 700 women, those with an ADHD diagnosis were over three times more likely to meet the screening criteria for PMDD.0 It used a screening questionnaire, not a clinical diagnosis, and it cannot show cause and effect.
    • At one ADHD clinic in the Netherlands, 45% of 209 women screened positive for PMDD symptoms, with no comparison group.0

    If you ever feel unsafe

    PMDD can bring dark thoughts for some women. If you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, call or text 988 right away. You are not alone, and it is treatable.

    ADHD rarely travels alone

    ADHD often shows up alongside other conditions, and each one can make the others harder to recognize and treat.0 You don’t need to diagnose yourself with any of these. Just know they are common, so you can mention anything that sounds familiar to your doctor.

    Anxiety

    About half of adults with ADHD also have an anxiety disorder.0

    Depression

    Depression is common alongside ADHD, and low mood can look a lot like the overwhelm of motherhood.0

    Sleep problems

    Trouble falling asleep, staying asleep or waking up rested is common in adults with ADHD.0

    Substance use

    Adults with ADHD have a higher rate of substance use problems, sometimes as a way of coping with symptoms that were never treated.0

    PMDD

    As you read above, women with ADHD may be more likely to have PMDD.0

    Why it matters

    Treating ADHD alone may not fix everything if something else is going on too. Bring the full picture to your appointment.

    After a baby

    In the Danish study from earlier in this chapter, ADHD diagnoses in mothers rose above pre-pregnancy levels two to five years after birth.0 The study can’t say why, but it’s one more reason the years after a baby deserve attention, not just the first few weeks.

    If your mood after a baby feels heavy, scary or not like you

    You don’t have to wait it out. The National Maternal Mental Health Hotline is free, confidential and open 24/7 by call or text: 1-833-TLC-MAMA (1-833-852-6262). It is not an emergency line: in an emergency, call 911, and in a mental health crisis, call or text 988.0

    Perimenopause

    • Perimenopause is the transition before menopause. It usually starts in your mid-to-late 40s, though some women notice changes in their late 30s. Menopause is confirmed after 12 months in a row without a period, and the average age in the US is 52.0
    • For women with ADHD, the research disagrees. In an Icelandic study of 5,392 women, 54.2% of those with ADHD reported debilitating perimenopause symptoms, compared with about a third of those without (ADHD was self-reported).0 A UK study of 656 women found no difference by ADHD diagnosis.0
    • A 2026 research review on ADHD medication for menopausal brain fog said it is too early to recommend treatments.0

    What to do with all of this

    • Track two cycles. Clinical guidance for doctors suggests tracking symptoms across two cycles before changing an ADHD medication dose.0 The tracker is on the next page.
    • Never change a medication dose on your own. Bring what you tracked to whoever prescribes it.
    • Be wary of anyone selling certainty. You’ll see plenty of products and clinics promising answers. Right now, the honest answer is that the research is early.
    • Lower the bar on purpose while you figure it out. Postpartum and perimenopause are seasons too. The self-care cards in Chapter 6 have minimum versions.

    The one thing to remember

    What is self-care, really?

    The word “self-care” is all the buzz right now. Shops use it to push their products and boost sales. A lot of us use it to justify a nightly glass of wine or a treat. But what is it? Why is it such a buzzword, and why does so much of this book come back to it?

    In plain terms, self-care means taking action to protect or improve your own health and well-being, especially during stressful times.

    My definition is: proactively making choices and building habits that not only help you sustain the life you have, or the life you want, but also push you into flourishing, not just surviving.

    There are a variety of levels of self-care, ranging from low-level, reactive self-care all the way up to deep, personal self-care that will move you into a healthier space than you can even imagine. What I find is that most people truly believe self-care is what I call the lowest level: the reactive kind. The kind that isn’t actually caring for yourself, but just numbing you and helping you escape the stress, anxiety, issues and problems you keep avoiding.

    Harsh? Maybe. True? Yes.

    The levels of self-care

    Vital self-care

    Doctor’s appointments and the dentist.

    “Treat yo self” self-care

    The (reactive) Snickers ice cream bar and a hot bath when the kids finally go down. Super fun, but not as vital.

    Fun self-care

    Hitting the salon, getting your nails done, shopping at a real makeup store instead of the drugstore. Let’s be honest: this is my favorite.

    Heart self-care

    Reading a really good book, and anything else that feeds your heart.

    Physical self-care

    Working out, eating well and staying hydrated.

    Deep self-care

    Healing from trauma and your past, noticing generational patterns, deciding the legacy you want to leave, learning to parent from a clean slate instead of from pain, and going to therapy to heal broken patterns.

    With a million other types in between.

    Mama, you can go for a walk, listen to a podcast, get ready, or do a million other things. But which will give you the biggest return? How do you prioritize? I’m sure that just reading that list, you’re wondering when the eff you actually have time for any of them.

    But proper self-care can help you keep up a sustainable life, no matter what season you are in.

    Proper self-care will sustain you and carry you into a brighter future. It gives you rest, energy and a mental break. It gives you a healthier, more hydrated body, so you actually feel good during the day. It gives you confidence and helps you be more productive.

    Self-care does look different in different seasons of life. Sometimes it’s free, like sitting alone in a room for 15 minutes so your mind has a chance to rest and decompress: a mini reset for yourself. Other times, you can use your money to buy some space, or a physical tool, to actually care for yourself. (And the right tools really do make a difference!)

    Low-level self-care is reactive, and it can be damaging when it becomes an excuse to only consume or escape, not sustain. Low-level self-care is temporary. It might give you a quick high, but it will fade just as quickly as it came.

    True self-care is proactive. The bottom line is that there is a big difference between treating yourself and sustaining yourself. Know the difference.

    The vase

    When I heard an analogy like this one, it stopped me in my tracks. It felt like a more complete picture of self-care, and of how vital it is for mothers. In her book Girl, Stop Apologizing, Rachel Hollis describes a woman as a glass vase being filled with water: everything she needs to live, like energy, love and joy.0 But instead of standing tall, we keep tipping ourselves this way and that, spilling a little onto our kids, our coworkers, our parents and our friends, until eventually the vase falls over and breaks. Her point: if the vase stands tall on a firm foundation and keeps taking in what is poured into it, it will eventually overflow onto everyone around it.

    So many women find themselves in this position. I like to call it “sacrificing yourself at the altar of motherhood”: spending so much effort trying to take care of everyone else that we destroy ourselves in the process. We destroy our mental health, our physical health, our relationships and our self-worth.

    Most moms and parents put their family’s needs first. As parents, we know that is necessary, sometimes. But what we know is that balance is possible, and it is earned. It takes being aware, prioritizing and fighting for peace in your motherhood. And there are so many ways to do that, from taking care of yourself, to managing your home in a way that is life-giving for you and your family, to staying aware of how life is going and being willing to make changes as needed.

    What moms actually want

    When mothers describe the self-care they want, it is often silence, boundaries and fewer obligations.21 Psychiatrist Pooja Lakshmin argues that real self-care is about boundaries, not bubble baths.22

    Why getting ready actually matters

    Hair and makeup are some of the most skipped-over, yet effective, habits in a woman’s day. Every woman’s morning routine or makeup look will be different, but the missed opportunity is the same.

    To some, it seems vain or selfish. But I challenge you to look at it from a different point of view.

    Getting in the habit of getting yourself ready in the first part of your day creates a spark that can carry productivity and confidence into the rest of it. I know the mere thought of adding one more thing to our to-do list is overwhelming for a lot of moms. We undervalue it because we think it will take too long, we are already too overwhelmed, or we believe it’s unimportant because “we just stay home.” Or maybe we don’t know how. Maybe no one ever taught us how to get ourselves ready. If you work outside the house, maybe you do get ready a little, but it takes too long, or you skip steps altogether to save time.

    The truth is, just like everything else, it takes time to build a habit. And once it’s a habit, you don’t have to think about it anymore. It’s automatic, just like brushing your teeth. We do that because we’ve made it something of value in our day. We need to value getting ready just as highly as brushing our teeth and putting clothes on each day, because it really can make a big difference in your day. Whether or not you want to believe it, your outside appearance does matter. Not in how people think of you, but in how YOU feel.

    Beauty isn’t fluffy. For me, it is tied to how I feel, mentally and emotionally. Getting ready is one of the quickest ways to get out of a funk and get an instant confidence boost. I’m more likely to leave the house and engage in conversation and activity than if I didn’t get ready. I even have a different demeanor when I talk with people, including my husband and my kids.

    Taking pride in your appearance isn’t vain unless you make it vain, by obsessing over it and letting it take priority over other important responsibilities. But using it as a tool is one of the smartest things you can do.

    Working out takes time to show results. Eating healthy takes time while your body adjusts. Going to therapy and doing deep internal work takes time and energy. I encourage you to do all of those. But I also encourage you to take 5 to 10 minutes to get yourself ready. You may see a shift in so many other areas when you take some pride in how you look (and feel).

    What success means in this season

    When it isn’t working

    Troubleshooting

    Getting to know yourself again isn’t a straight line. Find the snag that sounds like yours.

    “I feel guilty taking time for me.”

    You aren’t alone. I feel it too, and I take the time anyway, because I’m a better mom after it. Read my quiet-time story in “What sustains you?” (Chapter 2) and Time that’s yours (Chapter 7).

    “I don’t know what I want anymore.”

    That’s more common than you think. Start with the self-discovery questions and What I noticed (Chapter 2), then Look back before you look ahead (Chapter 8).

    “I can’t find any time.”

    Start with Finding time for me (Chapter 7), and use the minimum versions in Chapter 6. Five minutes in the car counts.

    “I slipped back into old patterns.”

    A slip isn’t failure. Restart. Go back to Patterns I want to keep, and patterns I want to break, and reread your truths (Chapter 3).

    “Every day feels like a bad day.”

    That’s more than a bad day. See “When a bad day turns into bad weeks” (Chapter 4), and talk to your doctor or a licensed therapist. In a crisis, call or text 988. If it might be ADHD or hormones, see Chapter 5.

    “My family isn’t on board.”

    Tell them you’re a person too, the way I did with my older kids (Chapter 2). Fill in your support map (Chapter 7). For sharing the load at home, see the companion workbook, Master Your Motherhood.
