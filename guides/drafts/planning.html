Embrace your strengths and your weaknesses

When you really understand and accept both your strengths and your weaknesses, you can move forward in a proactive way. Instead of beating yourself up for what you are not good at, you remember that you are human. You are good at some things and not so good at others, just like everyone else. That doesn’t mean you can’t get better. It just means your strengths and weaknesses become facts you can plan around, not proof that something is wrong with you.

For example, if you are not great at waking up early to “start your day off right,” then don’t force it just yet. Build your day around the time when YOU have the most energy and feel most focused and motivated. That might sound like: “While I might not be super productive at 5am like I want to be one day, I know right now that at 8pm I get an instant boost of energy and creativity once the kids are down for bed.” Then put your most important work in that window.

The same goes for everything else. If you are a great big-picture planner but terrible at remembering appointments, stop relying on your memory and let a shared calendar do it. If you are great with the kids in the morning but running on empty by dinner, plan easy dinners and save the hard conversations for earlier in the day. Work with how you are made, not against it.

When you stop fighting your weaknesses and start planning around them, you can move forward in a productive, proactive way that fits the season you are in. And when your season changes, your strengths and weaknesses may change with it. That is okay too.

Why a missed day doesn’t ruin it

What the research says

In a well-known study of how habits form, missing a single day did not significantly set people back. It took a median of 66 days for a new daily habit to feel automatic, with a huge range: from 18 days to an estimated 254.24

So if you skip a day, or a week, you haven’t broken anything. You restart. Restarting is part of the routine, not proof that you failed at it.
